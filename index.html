DIY Submarine — A low-cost PVC-hull underwater vehicle

Underwater vehicles are usually expensive and closed. We set out to build one from parts any lab or hardware shop has: a sealed uPVC pipe hull with a threaded, removable access cap, small DC-motor thrusters sealed in PVC sleeves, and a tethered cable that carries power and control from the surface. The hull was modelled in SolidWorks, assembled in the Makerspace and float-tested in a water tank.

SEC 02

The problem, and why we built it

Inspecting what is under water — tanks, wells, ponds, canals, ship hulls — is hard, risky and, with commercial ROVs, expensive.

Commercial remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) cost far more than a student lab or a small inspection job can justify, and they are closed systems that are hard to learn from. Open hobby projects show that a pipe-hull vehicle can be built from plumbing parts. Our goal in the Makerspace was to build one ourselves, end to end: design the hull in CAD, seal it, add propulsion, wire it from the surface, and test it in water — learning buoyancy, sealing and propulsion by doing.

The question we are answering

Can a student team build a watertight, controllable underwater vehicle from off-the-shelf uPVC pipe fittings and small DC motors — and what ballast, sealing and thrust does it need to go from floating to controlled diving?

SEC 03

How it is meant to work

Buoyancy. A sealed, air-filled pipe displaces a lot of water for its weight, so on its own it floats — which is exactly what the first tank test shows. Archimedes' principle sets the upward force: buoyancy equals the weight of the water the hull displaces. To dive, the vehicle's total weight must be brought close to that buoyancy (neutral buoyancy) by adding ballast, so the thrusters only need to push it up or down a little.

Trim. Where the ballast sits matters as much as how much there is. Weight placed low and centred keeps the hull level and stops it rolling; weight towards one end tips the nose up or down.

Propulsion and control. Small DC motors in sealed PVC sleeves drive propellers. Power and switching stay on the surface and reach the motors through a tether cable, so no battery or electronics need to be sealed inside the hull for this prototype. Reversing motor polarity reverses thrust.

Sealing. One end of the hull is permanently capped; the other has a threaded adapter and screw-in plug, so the hull can be opened for ballast or internal parts and closed again. Every cable entry and joint is a potential leak and is sealed.

Why "it floats" is a result, not a failure

A floating first test confirms the hull is closed and buoyant and shows how much ballast is needed before diving. Use the buoyancy lab below to estimate it from your own hull measurements.

SEC 05

Engineering journey

How the prototype was built, in order, with the real lab photographs and test footage. Click any photo to enlarge it.

  1. STEP 01

    CAD model in SolidWorks

    The hull was modelled first: a long cylindrical pipe body with an end cap on one end and the threaded adapter-and-plug access cap on the other, plus a mount for the motor at the access end. Working in CAD let us check the proportions and where parts attach before cutting anything.

  2. STEP 02

    Hull, access cap and thruster pods

    The hull is white uPVC pipe. One end takes a grey threaded adapter with a white screw-in plug — the removable access hatch. Small DC motors were housed in short PVC sleeves, the open ends packed with white sealant, with red four-blade propellers on the shafts.

    • Two motor pods are visible during fitting.
    • Motor leads leave each pod through the sealant.
  3. STEP 03

    Strapping, wiring and tether routing

    Wire ties were wrapped around the hull at several stations to hold the motor pods and to route the cable along the hull without loose loops. The motor leads were joined to the tether that runs back to the surface for power and switching.

  4. STEP 04

    First tank test

    The vehicle was lowered into a water tank. It floats horizontally at the surface with the top of the hull above the waterline, the thruster pod strapped underneath and the tether running to the tank edge. This confirms a closed, strongly buoyant hull and sets the next task: ballast and trim for diving.

  5. STEP 05

    Second iteration: CAD update, printed propeller, laser-cut parts

    After the float test the SolidWorks model was updated to the as-built layout — two thruster pods with propellers under the hull — and the custom parts moved to digital fabrication: a five-blade propeller printed on the Bambu Lab A1 mini and flat cradles and fins cut on the Makerspace laser cutter. See Digital fabrication.

Material choice: why uPVC

Pressure-rated uPVC pipe and fittings are cheap, rigid, corrosion-proof, easy to cut and drill, and solvent-cement into watertight joints. The threaded cleanout-style plug gives a reusable opening without cutting the hull. The trade-off is buoyancy: a large, air-filled pipe floats strongly and needs ballast to dive.

SEC 07

Digital fabrication: laser cutting and 3D printing

The first prototype used off-the-shelf plumbing parts and bought propellers. For the next iteration the custom parts come straight out of the SolidWorks model and are made on the two Makerspace machines below: flat parts on the CO2 laser cutter, shaped parts on the Bambu Lab A1 mini 3D printer. Settings marked To record will be filled in from the machine logs.

Process 01

Laser cutting — flat parts

The Makerspace CO2 laser cutter (SIL Lasers flat-bed machine) cuts 2D profiles directly from the SolidWorks sketches. Each flat part is exported as a DXF from the face of the SolidWorks part, so the cut outline is always the same as the CAD model.

Parts planned for the laser

  • Motor-pod cradles — saddle-shaped plates that sit between the pod and the hull so the straps do not let the pod rotate.
  • Stabiliser fins — flat plates clamped under the hull so the vehicle runs straight instead of yawing.
  • Internal ballast tray — a strip that slides into the hull through the threaded cap and holds the ballast weights low and centred.

Process 02

3D printing — Bambu Lab A1 mini

The bought red four-blade propellers are being replaced by our own design: a five-blade propeller modelled in SolidWorks (Submarine_Propeller_60mm_5mm.SLDPRT), exported as STL, sliced in Bambu Studio and printed on the Bambu Lab A1 mini. Designing the propeller ourselves lets us change diameter, pitch and blade count and re-print the same day.

Why a printed propeller

  • Hub bored to fit the motor shaft directly — no adapter needed.
  • Blade pitch can be tuned for thrust at low motor speed under water.
  • A spare set costs a few grams of filament.

From CAD to machine — the workflow

  1. Model in SolidWorks. Every custom part lives in the hull assembly so clearances to the pipe, caps and pods are checked before anything is made.
  2. Export. Flat parts → DXF for the laser; shaped parts → STL for the printer.
  3. Prepare. Nest the DXF on the laser bed; place and slice the STL in Bambu Studio.
  4. Make and check. Cut or print, then test-fit on the hull. Note the settings in fabrication-log.md.
  5. Iterate. Change the dimension in SolidWorks, export again, re-make. The CAD file stays the single source of truth.

Water test pending. PLA is fine for short tank tests; it softens in hot water and slowly absorbs moisture, so PETG is the fallback for longer use.

Next test plan

Leak test

Hold the closed hull fully under water for a set time; open and check for water inside. Repeat after every reseal.

Ballast & trim

Add weight in steps low in the hull until it just floats level, then trim end to end.

Thrust & speed

Time a run over a marked distance in the tank; note supply voltage.

Dive & surface

Check the thrusters can take the trimmed hull down and bring it back up.

Electrical

Measure motor current at the surface supply; check the tether for heating.

Log readings in data/tank-test-log-template.csv. Full protocol: testing-and-results.md.

Safety

  • Keep the surface power supply, switches and battery away from the tank and dry; use low voltage only.
  • Disconnect power before handling the vehicle, propellers or wiring.
  • Keep fingers clear of the propellers when powered.
  • Never connect mains power to the tether.
  • Dry and inspect seals, cable entries and the threaded cap after every wet test.
